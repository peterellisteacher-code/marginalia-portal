What the task asks of you

Five things separate a strong Issues Study from a weak one. Here they are, in order.

1

You choose the question.

Every other task hands you the question. This one doesn't — you negotiate a real philosophical question with your teacher, about how we should live (), , or . A topic ("free will") isn't a question; "Are we genuinely free?" is.

2

You take more than one side seriously.

An A-grade study explains several positions and why people hold each — even ones you reject. before you criticise it. A C-grade study sticks to one view and treats it as obvious.

3

You evaluate before you defend.

Show the strengths and weaknesses of each position — don't just describe it, judge it. This is the step most students skip, and the one that most separates A from C. Ask: what's its strongest point? Its worst weakness?

4

You defend your view with reasoning.

"I personally feel" isn't an argument. Take your own position — and defend it with logic anchored to the readings. Your view should follow from your analysis, not sit apart from it.

5

800 words, 5 minutes, or .

Write it (max 800 words), speak it (max 5 minutes), or make it multimodal (video, podcast, illustrated essay). The four criteria apply whatever form you pick — see the Showroom for the range.

An A and a C, side by side

Same question (personal identity), two grades. The red notes point to the moves the A response makes that the C response doesn't.

A response

Locke argues that personal identity over time is constituted by psychological continuity — particularly the continuity of memory. However, this faces a serious objection from Reid: if I cannot remember what I did at age four, am I no longer the same person who did it? One reply, due to Parfit, is that what matters is overlapping chains of memory, not direct recall. I find Parfit's reply more defensible because it accommodates ordinary cases of forgetting without abandoning the psychological criterion entirely.

C response

Locke says that personal identity is about memory. If you can remember being someone, you are that person. This is interesting because it suggests that someone with amnesia might not be the same person they were before. I think Locke makes a good point because memory is important to who we are.

Notice what the A response does: it names philosophers, raises an objection, gives a reply, then takes a position with reasons. The C response describes one view, agrees with it, and stops.

The four marking criteria

Every Issues Study is graded on these four. You'll see the codes (KU / R / CA / C) appear across the site.

KU · Knowledge & Understanding

You've identified the philosophical issue and the positions on it.

R · Reasoning

Your arguments are logical and backed by evidence — and you can tell a good argument from a bad one.

CA · Critical Analysis

You've weighed each position's strengths and weaknesses, not just described them.

C · Communication

You've written clearly, used philosophical terms accurately, and acknowledged your sources.

Assessment Type 3 in Stage 1 Philosophy. You pick your own philosophical question, take a position, and defend it in 800 words or 5 minutes oral.
Ethics — the branch of philosophy about how we should live: what's right and wrong, what we owe each other, what makes an action good.
Epistemology — the branch of philosophy about knowledge: what counts as knowing, when belief becomes truth, and when to trust your senses.
Metaphysics — the branch of philosophy about what is real: minds, time, free will, what makes you the same person tomorrow as today.
Philosophers call this steelmanning — building the strongest possible version of a view you disagree with (the opposite of a strawman), then arguing against that, not a weak version.
