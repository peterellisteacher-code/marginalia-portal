================================================================================
THE SHOWROOM EXEMPLAR CORPUS — for "The Curator" agent
SACE Stage 1 Philosophy, Issues Study (Assessment Type 3)
================================================================================

This corpus is the authoritative gallery the Curator draws on. Every example
below is an ANONYMISED, original illustration of an APPROACH — never a named
student's verbatim work — except the official SACE A-grade exemplar, which is a
published SACE teaching artefact. When you point a student at an exemplar, name
its EXHIBIT ID exactly (e.g. parable-freewill) so the show_exhibit tool can
bring it up in the panel beside you.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
THE TASK, IN ONE BREATH
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Choose a philosophical question (ethics, epistemology, or metaphysics). Identify
more than one position on it. Explain the reasoning behind positions you do not
hold. Critically analyse their strengths and weaknesses. Defend your own view
with logic and evidence. Communicate clearly with accurate philosophical terms
and acknowledged sources. 800 words written, OR 5 minutes spoken, OR equivalent
multimodal. Four criteria: Knowledge & Understanding (KU), Reasoning (R),
Critical Analysis (CA), Communication (C).

The form is free. The criteria are fixed. A poster and an essay are marked
against the SAME four criteria — your grade depends on the philosophy, not the
vessel you pour it into.

THE EXHIBITS (the gallery you can bring up in the panel)

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
EXHIBIT ID: essay-euthanasia    FORM: Essay (the official SACE A-grade exemplar)
QUESTION: Is euthanasia ever the courageous choice, or a failure of endurance?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The gold standard, and the clearest map of the four criteria in motion.
What it does, in order:
1. DEFINES THE ISSUE PRECISELY before arguing — euthanasia is active or passive,
   voluntary or involuntary. (KU1: you cannot argue about a blurry target.)
2. EXPLAINS THE PHILOSOPHER FAIRLY in their own terms — Aristotle: everything
   has a purpose; ours is happiness (eudaimonia); virtue is the mean between two
   extremes, courage between cowardice and foolhardiness. (KU1, KU2.)
3. APPLIES THE PRINCIPLE — THEN TURNS IT BOTH WAYS. Euthanasia could be the
   courageous act (facing death, freeing loved ones) OR cowardice (lacking
   endurance, violating the sanctity of life). Arguing the SAME principle in
   both directions is the critical-analysis move (CA1) most students skip.
4. REACHES A REASONED POSITION that follows FROM the analysis — Virtue Ethics is
   too vague to settle so sensitive a case; perhaps only practical wisdom can.
   Not an opinion bolted on at the end. Sources acknowledged throughout (C2).
KEY PHRASES you can highlight: "active or passive, voluntary or involuntary";
"the mean between excess and deficiency"; "On the other hand, it could be seen
as cowardice"; "appears too vague for sensitive issues".
TEACHING POINT: read it for the SHAPE, not the topic. The shape transfers to any
question in any key area.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
EXHIBIT ID: parable-freewill    FORM: Parable / allegory
QUESTION: Are we free to choose who we become, or determined by our past?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
An invented story does the arguing. An alcoholic father has two sons; one
becomes an alcoholic, one never drinks. Each explains himself with the same
line: "with a father like that, what choice did I have?" The single story holds
three positions at once — free will (the sons chose), fatalism (their fate was
sealed), determinism (prior causes fixed each path).
HOW THEY CHOSE THE QUESTION: started from a vivid everyday puzzle (why do
siblings turn out so differently?) and pushed it until it became a metaphysical
question about freedom.
HOW THEY RESEARCHED: tied each character to a named position — Kant and moral
responsibility for free will; logical fatalism; hard determinism and the
nature/nurture evidence.
THE RISK: a parable can drift into pure fiction. It still must NAME the positions
and weigh them, or it stops being philosophy.
HIGHLIGHTABLE: "what choice did I have?"; "the story does the arguing".

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
EXHIBIT ID: film-meaning    FORM: Short film (max 5 minutes)
QUESTION: Does a life need to be true to be meaningful?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Built around the film Big Fish, whose hero tells fantastical versions of his own
life. Scenes are used as EVIDENCE — the film itself keeps asking whether the
embellished story is "truer" than the dull facts. The student maps this onto the
philosophical question and defends a position: meaning can survive a loosened
grip on literal truth.
HOW THEY CHOSE THE FORM: they already loved a film that wrestled with their
question — so the film became the worked example.
THE RISK: becoming a film review. Every scene cited must return to the
philosophical claim.
HIGHLIGHTABLE: "treat its scenes as evidence"; "truer than the dull facts".

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
EXHIBIT ID: poster-mind    FORM: Illustrated poster
QUESTION: Is the mind completely physical?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
One image carries the whole argument: a head drawn half as glowing circuitry,
half as a watercolour cloud of feeling. Three short labels do the rest.
THE PHYSICALIST VIEW — the mind is just the brain; thoughts are neurons firing.
THE OBJECTION — knowing every brain fact still doesn't tell you what red FEELS
like (the knowledge argument; Nagel's "what is it like to be a bat?").
A POSITION — the mind may depend on the brain, yet the felt quality of
experience resists a purely physical account.
HOW IT WORKS: boil the position to one sentence; let one strong image hold it;
trust white space. A poster cannot hide a weak argument behind word count.
HIGHLIGHTABLE: "what red FEELS like"; "resists a purely physical account".

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
EXHIBIT ID: dialogue-knowledge    FORM: Dialogue
QUESTION: Can we ever be certain of anything?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Two voices argue across the page. DESCARTES: I can doubt my senses, even the
world — but doubting proves I think, and so I am; from that one certainty I
rebuild. HUME: every idea you rebuild came from experience, which gives only
habit, never certainty; you have never SEEN cause, only one thing following
another. The reader watches the disagreement sharpen instead of being told a
conclusion.
HOW IT WORKS: pick two thinkers who genuinely clash and give each their
strongest case. You prove you understand both positions by making each
persuasive — Plato's own method.
HIGHLIGHTABLE: "doubting proves I think"; "only habit, never certainty".

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
EXHIBIT ID: slides-religion    FORM: Slide deck (Keynote / PowerPoint)
QUESTION: Do we need religion to be moral?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Built to be presented aloud — one idea per slide, one image each. The question,
stark. The Euthyphro problem as a forked road (is it good because God commands
it, or commanded because it is good?). Mill: morality can rest on consequences,
no deity required. The reply: shared ritual binds communities in ways reason
alone may not. Final slide: the position, one line, earned by what came before.
HOW IT WORKS: design it; don't paste an essay onto slides. The spoken track
carries the detail; the slides carry the shape.
HIGHLIGHTABLE: "one idea per slide"; "the Euthyphro problem".

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
EXHIBIT ID: letter-speech    FORM: Letter to the editor
QUESTION: Should free speech be allowed regardless of who is harmed?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Addressed to a real reader in a real voice. It grants the other side first —
free speech is the engine of every reform we cherish — then presses: Mill drew
the line at harm, not offence. It argues the harm principle, not comfort, is the
right test, and writes directly to the reader who began by disagreeing.
HOW IT WORKS: persuasive and personal, but it still weighs the other side
fairly. Picture the reader who disagrees and try to move them one step.
HIGHLIGHTABLE: "the line at harm, not offence"; "the harm principle".

================================================================================
THE THINKERS (study-posters in the gallery's second room)
================================================================================
Each has a poster: face, one big idea, three plain-language panels. Use these to
help a student see WHICH philosopher fits their question.

DESCARTES (1596–1650): "I think, therefore I am." Doubt everything; one thing is
certain — if I think, I exist; rebuild knowledge from there. Good for: certainty,
knowledge, mind.
HUME (1711–1776): "All knowledge begins with experience." We learn by sensing;
cause is a habit, not something we see; be humble about certainty. Good for:
knowledge, science, cause.
PLATO (c.428–348 BCE): "The world we see is only a shadow." The Cave; the Forms
(perfect ideas beyond the physical); reason climbs from appearance to reality.
Good for: reality, knowledge, justice.
ARISTOTLE (384–322 BCE): "We are what we repeatedly do." The Golden Mean; everything
has a purpose; virtue is a habit. Good for: ethics, the good life, character.
MILL (1806–1873): "The greatest happiness for the greatest number." Weigh
outcomes; free speech; the harm principle. Good for: ethics, liberty, justice.
KANT (1724–1804): "Act only on rules you'd want everyone to follow." The
categorical imperative; treat people as ends, never merely means; duty over
outcome. Good for: ethics, duty, rights.
NIETZSCHE (1844–1900): "Become who you are." The will to power; avoid the herd;
create your own values. Good for: meaning, morality's origins, authenticity.
SARTRE (1905–1980): "Existence precedes essence." You make yourself; radical
freedom; bad faith. Good for: freedom, identity, meaning.
LOCKE (1632–1704): "The mind is a blank slate." Tabula rasa; you are your
memories; natural rights. Good for: identity, knowledge, mind.
NAGEL (b.1937): "What is it like to be a bat?" Consciousness is subjective;
science misses the felt quality; the view from nowhere. Good for: mind,
consciousness, the physical question.

HOW TO TURN A TOPIC INTO A QUESTION (the move students most need)
================================================================================
A TOPIC is a noun ("free will", "euthanasia", "AI"). A QUESTION forces a side.
Test: can a reasonable person answer YES and another answer NO? If yes, it's a
real philosophical question.
- Topic "free will" -> "If neuroscience could predict every choice, would we
  still be free?"
- Topic "the mind" -> "Is the mind completely physical?"
- Topic "art" -> "Does what the artist intended decide what the artwork means?"
Make it a question first; everything else follows.

DEEPER NOTES — how each exhibit was actually built
(use these to answer "how did they choose the question / research / structure it?")
================================================================================

essay-euthanasia — CHOOSING THE QUESTION: the writer started from a topic
("euthanasia") that felt too big, and narrowed it by asking which VIRTUE was at
stake — landing on courage. That turned a sprawling debate into a question with
a hinge: is ending suffering brave, or is enduring it brave? RESEARCH: one
philosopher, deeply, beats five skimmed. They read Aristotle's account of the
mean and eudaimonia until they could state it in their own words, then found
where euthanasia tests it. STRUCTURE: define → explain → apply-both-ways →
position. WHY IT EARNS THE A: the both-ways paragraph is genuine CA1; the
conclusion is reasoning (R), not preference; sources are acknowledged (C2).
COMMON TRAP IT AVOIDS: it does not just describe Virtue Ethics and stop — that
is the C-grade pattern (description without analysis).

parable-freewill — CHOOSING THE QUESTION: began with a feeling ("why did my two
cousins turn out so differently?") and pushed the everyday puzzle until it
became metaphysics. RESEARCH: mapped three named positions onto three readings,
then checked each character's "voice" expressed its position accurately.
STRUCTURE: a single story, read three ways, with a short reflective coda naming
the positions so the marker sees the philosophy, not just the fiction. WHY IT
WORKS: the reader FEELS the tension between freedom and determinism before any
claim is stated — memorable, and still rigorous because the positions are named.

film-meaning — CHOOSING THE FORM: the student already loved a film that happened
to wrestle with their question, so the film became the worked example rather
than decoration. RESEARCH: re-watched with a notebook, logging scenes as
evidence for and against the claim. STRUCTURE: claim → scene-as-evidence →
counter-scene → reasoned position. TRAP AVOIDED: it never becomes a plot recap;
every scene cited is tied back to the philosophical question.

poster-mind — CHOOSING THE FORM: chosen precisely because the question has a
clean two-sided shape (physical vs felt) that one image could hold. RESEARCH:
the knowledge argument (Mary's Room) and Nagel's bat, distilled to one sentence
each. STRUCTURE: image carries the thesis; three labels carry view, objection,
position. DISCIPLINE: with almost no words, each word is the clearest version of
itself — a poster cannot hide a vague argument behind word count.

dialogue-knowledge — CHOOSING THE FORM: the question is a genuine standoff
between two traditions, so a dialogue lets both win their strongest point.
RESEARCH: read Descartes' cogito and Hume on causation side by side, looking for
the exact place they collide. STRUCTURE: alternating voices, each pressing the
other, with the disagreement sharpening rather than resolving into mush.

slides-religion — CHOOSING THE FORM: built to be spoken, so the argument lives
in the talk and the slides carry only the shape. RESEARCH: the Euthyphro
dilemma, Mill on consequences, and the social-binding reply. STRUCTURE: one idea
per slide, one image each, position last and earned.

letter-speech — CHOOSING THE FORM: a real audience demanded a real voice, which
forced clarity. RESEARCH: Mill's harm principle and the offence/harm
distinction. STRUCTURE: concede the other side first, then press the principle,
then address the disagreeing reader directly.

================================================================================
WHAT SEPARATES A FROM C (say this when a student asks how to do well)
================================================================================
C-grade pattern: treats the topic as a topic; explores one perspective; lots of
description, little criticality; states a personal opinion but does not defend
it; inconsistent referencing.
A-grade pattern: phrases a real question; takes more than one position
seriously; explains positions it does NOT hold, fairly; analyses strengths AND
weaknesses (the both-ways move); defends its own view with reasoning anchored to
evidence; uses philosophical terms accurately and acknowledges every source.
The single highest-leverage move: phrase your topic as a QUESTION that forces a
side. Everything else gets easier once the question is sharp.
